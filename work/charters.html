Test Charters: Tripling Output by Subtraction

A one-sentence contract for structured exploration. When demand roughly doubled and headcount went down, this is what scaled — not a new tool, a smaller process.

Adapted from an internal framework I introduced and ran with my team for years. The examples are genericized; the mechanism — and the numbers — are real.

🔥 The tl;dr: a charter is plan, execution, and report in one document — general enough to cover wildly different kinds of investigation, specific enough to keep the exploration pointed at one thing. Everything else this page describes is what we deleted to make room for it.

The problem: a process that outgrew its purpose

The starting state will be familiar to anyone who's run a small team inside a growing org: demand climbing, headcount flat (then down), and a documentation-and-ceremony stack that had accreted over years. Plans in one artifact, execution notes in another, reports in a third — each with its own template, each requiring its own upkeep, none of them read as often as they were written.

Everyone's instinct — including the people asking me how we'd cope — was tooling. Add automation, add dashboards, add process. I audited the stack instead, with one question per artifact and ceremony: does this actually move the investigation forward, or does it just record that we investigated?

The move: subtraction

The answer to the audit was a short kill-list, and one replacement. We collapsed the plan/execution/report stack into a single living document — the charter — and cut the ceremonies that existed to shuttle information between the three.

The charter's spine is one sentence:

EXPLORE [target] AS [a persona] USING [resources and heuristics] TO DISCOVER [information].

That sentence is a contract. It scopes the work (one target, not "everything"), declares the lens (whose experience are we simulating?), names the toolkit (which heuristics apply), and — most importantly — states what we're trying to learn, not what we're trying to confirm. Exploration without a discovery clause drifts; exploration with one ends when the question is answered.

Findings, blockers, and the debrief all live in the same document, appended as the work happens. When the charter closes, the report already exists — because it was never a separate artifact.

The result

Structured-exploration output tripled year-over-year — a verified figure from our own tracking data, not a vibe — while the team got smaller and demand kept growing. Burnout went down, not up: the ceremony we cut was exactly the part of the job people dreaded.

The charters themselves also got better with age. Late-period charters ran multi-day investigations with per-item status, embedded evidence, and multi-round iteration — the format grew into a lightweight project-management tool without ever growing a second template.

Why this generalizes

Nothing in the mechanism is specific to testing. It's a general answer to a general problem: scaling judgment-driven work when you can't scale the team.

  • Constraint over ceremony. A one-sentence scope beats a ten-page plan when the work is exploratory — the plan can't know what the exploration will find.
  • One artifact, whole lifecycle. Any handoff between plan, doing, and reporting is a place where information dies. Collapse them.
  • A discovery clause. "To discover X" is what separates an investigation from wandering. It's also what tells you when you're done.
  • Subtraction is a design act. Deleting process is harder than adding it — every ceremony has a defender — and it compounds just as hard in the other direction.

The open question I keep chewing on: what does a deliberately smaller process look like in a research org, where the unit of work is a hypothesis and human attention is the scarcest resource in the building? I suspect the answer looks a lot like a charter.
