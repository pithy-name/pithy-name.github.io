Heuristics: Judgment Under Uncertainty

A field guide to making good judgment calls when exhaustive analysis isn't on the menu — with names silly enough that you'll actually remember them.

Adapted from an internal framework I wrote for my team. The examples are genericized; the heuristics — and their names — are the originals.

☝️ You probably have experience forming an educated guess, making a gut call, using a rule of thumb, or plain common sense. These are ways of making a judgment call, knowing full well that your judgment is fallible. They are heuristics!

What are heuristics?

Heuristics are tools that help us do the right thing in a given situation, exercising our discretion and judgment. We accept a heuristic's fallibility as a reasonable trade-off — a good-enough answer now beats a perfect answer never.

They matter anywhere you're exploring a system too big to check exhaustively: probing software, pressure-testing a plan, debugging an org process, poking at any black box. You can't try everything. Heuristics tell you which pokes are worth it.

When to apply heuristics

We use heuristics when we need to decide what, how, or whether to do a thing. "Should I do this thing?" A rule of thumb helps you decide.

As an exploration strategy: exhaustive searches are impractical, and without a frame the scope is hopelessly open-ended. Heuristics give the exploration structure — and you can deliberately diversify by applying contradictory heuristics to the same problem.

Macro-heuristics

The big frames — where to point your attention in the first place.

  1. Goldilocks Heuristic: boundary thinking — too big, too small, just right. Too big = fifteen thousand saved items; too small = zero or one; just right = a few comfortable pages' worth. Check all three.
  2. The Perimeter Assumption: assume the happy path is OK if the boundaries are OK.
  3. CRUD: Create, Read, Update, Delete — every piece of state has a lifecycle. Walk it end to end.
  4. Consistency: is the system consistent across contexts, surfaces, and flows? We recognize most problems as inconsistencies with something we expected.
  5. Sanity Check: do things make sense?
  6. The Soap Opera: ex.a.gger.ate every aspect of a scenario based on a real-life use case.
  7. The Smoke Test: where there's 🚬 there's 🔥 — a fast, shallow pass over everything before a deep dive into anything.
  8. The Pimple Heuristic: poke it until it pops or you're satisfied nothing interesting will happen.
  9. Ifs are Iffy: explore the places where conditionals live. Every "if" is a fork someone might not have finished thinking through.
  10. Yellow is Interesting: the middle ground where it's not just binary. Explore the factors leading into and out of the in-between state — then check the yes and the no, too.
  11. If you can touch it, it's real: just seeing it isn't enough. Click the new thing to make sure it's actually there. And if you see something that shouldn't be there — click that too.
  12. The Familiar Problem: you've seen this shape before. Trust the déjà vu enough to investigate it.
  13. The Availability Heuristic: what comes to mind first isn't necessarily what matters most — notice when recency is doing your prioritizing for you.
  14. Break the Chain: interrupt a multi-step process in the middle and see what survives.

Micro-heuristics

These are the heuristics you apply when thinking: what's next? They earn their whimsical names — a heuristic you can't remember is a heuristic you won't use.

🍞 BSG: All of this has happened before and all of it will happen again 🍞

  • Look for problems that have occurred in the past — possibly in a different part of the system.
  • Keep a running catalog of past failures and revisit it to refresh your memory. History doesn't repeat, but it rhymes constantly.

😈 WWGD: What Would a Gremlin Do? 😈

  • Try the other things on the screen. Play devil's advocate.
  • Even if it's not meant to be used a certain way — if someone can do it, they will do it. So try it first.
  • Example: jump straight to a deep step of a flow you're not supposed to be able to reach.

🏝️ LOST: We have to go back! 🏝️

  • Undo and redo are notoriously tricky to build. Moving back and forth between steps can lose data.
  • Look for every way to retrace your steps, and take them all. Go back with the browser button and the in-app button. They are not the same trip.

🛶 Go with the Flow 🛶

  • Follow a flow from beginning to end — no shortcuts, no assumptions about the middle.

🏁 No Time to Lose! 🏁

  • Be impatient: act in very quick succession. Challenge the system to keep up with you.
  • Example: interact with a list before it has finished loading.

🔱 The Devil Lies in the Details 🔎

  • Keep an eye out for the finer details — the copy, the alignment, the off-by-one. Small wrongness is often the visible tip of large wrongness.

The Limit Does Not Exist

  • Push past where the system claims to stop. Maximums, minimums, "that never happens" — verify the ceiling personally.

How to identify a heuristic

⚒️ Where do you get your crazy ideas from? Anyone who explores systems carries a mental toolbox of ideas fabricated through intuition. Each tool in that toolbox is a heuristic.

We subconsciously use heuristics every day. Pairing with a colleague can help us recognize and articulate them.

  1. Identify patterns in how you probe a problem.
  2. Think about why you make those particular checks.
  3. Think about why you performed those actions in that order.
  4. Give it a cutesy name to make it easy to remember, and write it down.
  5. Tada — done!

Consider: when something doesn't meet "expectations" — where do the expectations come from?

Expectations revolve around desirable consistencies between related things. In general, we recognize a problem when we observe that a system is inconsistent with one or more of those principles: we expect this, we get that, and now we have reason to suspect a problem. Naming which expectation was violated is also what makes a report credible — decision-makers can see exactly why you believe an observation points to a problem.
