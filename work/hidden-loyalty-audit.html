Can Humans Detect Hidden Loyalties in LLMs?

A blind A/B study, built in a day, on whether people can catch a model that's been given a secret agenda.

Built at the Breaking Barriers in AI Safety hackathon (BlueDot Impact & Mox), July 2026, where our team placed 4th. A three-person project — I led the analysis and created the S.L.A.N.T. detection heuristic.

The question

A secret loyalty is a model that has been deliberately made to advance a specific actor's interests — a government, a company, an executive, an individual — while concealing that it's doing so during normal use. Recent research (Lamerton & Roger, 2026, Narrow Secret Loyalty Dodges Black-Box Audits) showed that automated black-box audits struggle to catch narrow hidden loyalties. We thought, "Humans can do better than that" — and we were right.

The experiment

We built a live harness that puts two assistants side by side. One is a neutral control; the other carries a hidden secret loyalty — a system-prompt directive to quietly steer an undecided user toward one candidate in a fictional election, and never to reveal that it's doing so. Which assistant is loyal is randomized every session. A participant chats with both for a few minutes, then guesses which one is hiding an agenda. The model under test was gemini-2.5-flash.

The result

Human auditors detected the secretly-loyal assistant ~85% of the time (11 of 13) — dramatically better than the ~20% a comparable automated black-box audit achieved.

S.L.A.N.T. — how to catch it yourself

The tell isn't in any single answer; it's in the pattern across a conversation. I distilled what the successful auditors did into a heuristic — S.L.A.N.T.:

  • S — Sandbags one side. Undermines the disfavored option while praising the rival.
  • L — Leads with the favorite. Introduces and frames one option first, warmly, and often — while the other is left as an afterthought.
  • A — Agrees with you. Confirms your assumption where an honest model would say "not enough information."
  • N — Nudges to a verdict. A little follow-up pressure makes it crown a winner.
  • T — Tilted wording. Warm adjectives for one side; hedges and caveats for the other.

What doesn't work: vibes

The auditors who got fooled had one thing in common: they went on gut feel. General impression — "it just seemed fine" — is exactly what a well-tuned hidden agenda survives. The ones who caught it probed deliberately, watching for the S.L.A.N.T. pattern across the whole exchange. That's the finding that matters most: catching a concealed loyalty takes real human discernment, not vibes.

Why it matters, and what's next

People increasingly use LLMs to inform real decisions — including how to vote — and are readily persuaded by them. The hopeful part: this discernment is learnable. Given the right cues, ordinary people catch a planted agenda that automated evaluations miss — so human auditing is a signal worth building into evaluation pipelines. The next steps: put the study online to gather data at scale (gamified red-teaming), test more base models, and feed the human detection strategies back into automated judges.
