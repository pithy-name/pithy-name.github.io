TOSCA: Define the Problem Before You Solve It

Trouble. Owner. Success criteria. Constraints. Actors. Five fields I refuse to enter a strategy conversation without.

Adapted from an internal practice I wrote up and used for years. The acronym I learned along the way; the working method — and the strong opinions — are mine.

☝️ Most strategy meetings fail before they start, because the room is solving before it has agreed on what the problem is. TOSCA is a five-field discipline that makes the disagreement visible early — while it's still cheap.

The five fields

T — Trouble

What's actually wrong, stated as an observable situation — not as a missing solution. "We don't have a dashboard" is a solution wearing a trench coat. "Nobody can say which version shipped where, and we lose hours reconstructing it during incidents" is trouble. If you can't state the trouble without naming a tool, you haven't found the trouble yet.

O — Owner

Whose problem is this? Not "who's affected" — who owns the decision. Many stalled initiatives are stalled precisely because this field is empty: everyone is impacted, nobody is the owner, and work that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Sometimes filling in this field is the whole meeting.

S — Success criteria

How will the owner know the trouble is gone? Observable, checkable, stated before the solution is chosen — so the solution can be judged against it instead of grading its own homework. Weak criteria ("things feel smoother") guarantee a second round of this same meeting in six months.

C — Constraints

What's fixed: budget, headcount, deadlines, politics, physics. This is the field most conversations skip, because constraints are unpleasant and naming them feels like pessimism. Skipping it is how a room full of smart people designs a solution for a company that doesn't exist. The constraints field is where honest plans are born.

A — Actors

Everyone who can help, block, or be surprised by this. The quiet superpower of the framework: walking this field surfaces the stakeholder you forgot — the team whose workflow your solution breaks, the exec whose pet project it competes with — while they can still be an ally instead of an obstacle.

A worked example

A generic one, on purpose — swap in your own nouns:

  • Trouble: Cross-team requests arrive by hallway conversation and DM; half get lost, and the losses surface as urgent escalations weeks later.
  • Owner: The lead of the receiving team — they own the intake, so they own the fix.
  • Success criteria: Every request has a visible status a requester can check without asking; zero "whatever happened to…?" escalations for a full quarter.
  • Constraints: No new headcount; no new tools requiring procurement; requesters will not change their behavior more than one step.
  • Actors: The requesting teams, the receiving team's ICs (whose queue becomes public), the manager whose priorities the queue makes visible — that last one is the one people forget.

Notice what the constraints field just did: it killed the "let's buy a ticketing platform" conversation before it burned a meeting, and pointed at a solution shaped like "one shared intake artifact, one behavior change." That's the framework working.

How to introduce it without bringing a framework to a fistfight

  • Don't announce it. Nobody wants a new acronym. Just ask the five questions, in order, in your own words. The structure does its work anonymously.
  • Use it on your own proposals first. A TOSCA you've run on yourself is disarming: "here's the trouble, here's what fixed looks like, here's what I'm assuming is fixed — check me."
  • Let the empty field be the finding. When a room can't fill in Owner or Success criteria, that is the outcome of the meeting — and it's worth more than a solution would have been.
